Florida Condo Milestone Inspections in 2026: What Every Longboat Key Buyer Needs to Know Before Closing

By Amanda LeGault  |  October 1, 2026

What does Florida's SB 4D milestone inspection law mean for Longboat Key condo buyers?

Florida's SB 4D law requires condo buildings three stories or taller that are 30 years or older to complete a structural milestone inspection. On Longboat Key, those inspections are actively underway in 2026 — and while most buildings are structurally sound, the costs of compliance are real. Before you make an offer on a Longboat Key condo, you need to know the building's inspection status, reserve funding percentage, and whether any special assessments are pending or on the horizon.

If you're shopping for a condo on Longboat Key right now, there's one question that cuts through everything else: Has this building had its milestone inspection — and what did it find?

Florida Senate Bill 4D changed the landscape of condo ownership across the state. Passed in 2022 in the wake of the Champlain Towers South collapse in Surfside, it set hard deadlines and clear requirements for structural inspections and reserve funding. On Longboat Key — where much of the waterfront condo inventory was built in the 1970s, '80s, and '90s — most buildings fall squarely in scope.

The good news: the Longboat Key Observer reported in April 2026 that inspections underway on the Key have generally found buildings to be in sound structural shape. That matters. But "structurally sound" and "no cost to you" are two very different things — and that gap is exactly where buyers can get caught off guard.

Here's what you need to know before you write an offer.

Phase 1 vs. Phase 2: What Each Inspection Actually Means

SB 4D creates a two-phase inspection process for covered buildings.

Phase 1 is a visual inspection conducted by a licensed engineer or architect. The inspector walks the building, evaluates structural components, and issues a written report. If everything looks sound, Phase 1 is the end of the road — the association files the report and moves on.

Phase 2 is triggered only if Phase 1 finds substantial structural deterioration. At that point, the engineer conducts a more invasive, detailed assessment — often including destructive testing of materials, more extensive documentation, and specific repair recommendations. Phase 2 buildings aren't automatically dangerous or uninsurable, but they do carry more uncertainty and often more cost.

When you're evaluating a specific building on Longboat Key, you want to know: Has it completed Phase 1? What did the report say? Is Phase 2 required? Has it been done? Those answers shape everything from your financing to your assessment risk going forward.

The SIRS Requirement: Reserve Funding Has Changed — Permanently

Beyond the milestone inspection itself, SB 4D introduced a second major requirement: the Structural Integrity Reserve Study, or SIRS.

A SIRS is an engineering study that quantifies the cost to repair and replace a building's critical structural components over the life of the structure — roof, load-bearing walls, foundation, plumbing, waterproofing, and more. Florida associations are now required to fund their reserves based on the SIRS, and they can no longer vote to "waive" reserves the way many associations did before 2022.

That's a significant change. For years, Florida condo associations routinely passed budgets that underfunded — or completely waived — reserve contributions to keep monthly fees low. Those savings created a deferred maintenance bill that eventually came due in the form of emergency repairs and special assessments.

SB 4D ended that. Now, associations must collect adequate reserves and hold them in designated accounts. For buyers, this means two things: monthly fees may be higher than they were two years ago, and the risk of a catastrophic, underfunded emergency is lower. Both of those outcomes are actually good for you as a buyer — even if the higher fee sticker looks unfamiliar at first.

What you want to verify is the association's current reserve funding percentage. A building that's 80–100% funded is in strong shape. A building that's below 50% funded may still be catching up to the new requirements — and that gap could surface as a special assessment if something needs repair before reserves are fully built up.

Special Assessments: How to Read the Risk Before You Buy

Special assessments are the word no condo buyer wants to hear. They're one-time charges assessed to all unit owners — sometimes in the tens of thousands of dollars — to cover repairs or improvements the association's reserves can't fully absorb.

Across Florida's condo market in 2025 and 2026, special assessments have risen significantly as associations complete inspections, address deferred maintenance, and fund reserve studies. Longboat Key is not immune. Even where buildings are structurally sound, the cost of completing inspections, addressing minor findings, updating reserve studies, and implementing the new reserve funding schedules can be substantial.

Here's what you need to request — and what it tells you:

Before You Make an Offer: Documents to Request

  • Milestone inspection report (Phase 1 and Phase 2 if applicable) — What did the engineer find? What repairs, if any, were required?
  • Structural Integrity Reserve Study (SIRS) — What does full reserve funding look like for this building, and where is the association today?
  • Current reserve funding percentage — Ask directly: what percentage funded are the reserves as of the last association statement?
  • Board meeting minutes — last 24 months — Voted assessments, discussed assessments, and repair conversations all show up here.
  • Certificate of Association Status — Confirms whether any pending or enacted assessments exist and whether the association is current on its statutory obligations.
  • Current budget and most recent annual financial statement — A healthy balance sheet tells you a lot about how the board is managing the building's money.

Under Florida law, sellers must disclose known pending special assessments in the contract. But "pending" has a legal definition — it means the board has voted on it. A discussion in a board meeting about a future assessment that hasn't been formally approved yet doesn't technically require disclosure. That's why the meeting minutes matter.

I pull these documents for every Longboat Key condo buyer I work with. There's too much real money on the table — at the $1M–$3M price points common on the Key — to skip this step.

What "Sound But Costly" Means for Your Closing

When the Observer reported in April 2026 that Longboat Key buildings are generally in sound structural shape, that's genuinely good news. It means the catastrophic scenario — a building deemed unsafe or requiring emergency evacuation — is not the story here.

But sound structure doesn't mean zero cost. The process of completing a milestone inspection, commissioning a SIRS, and moving to full reserve funding creates real carrying costs for associations — and those costs flow through to unit owners in the form of monthly assessment increases, special assessments for past-due maintenance items, or both.

For buyers in the $1M–$3M range, you can typically absorb modest fee increases or small assessments. What you want to avoid is buying into a building with a large, unaddressed deferred maintenance bill — where the inspection is done but the repair plan isn't funded and the reserves aren't there to cover it.

That's the specific due diligence question. Not "is this building safe?" but "is this association financially prepared to address what the inspection found?"

Financing a Longboat Key Condo in 2026: What Lenders Now Require

This is a part of the process that surprises buyers — especially those using conventional financing (Fannie Mae or Freddie Mac-backed loans).

After Surfside, both Fannie and Freddie updated their condo project approval standards to account for structural integrity. For loans on individual units in buildings subject to SB 4D requirements, lenders may require documentation that the association is in compliance — including confirmation that the milestone inspection has been completed and that reserves are being funded per SIRS requirements.

If a building is significantly behind on its inspection or is substantially underfunded on reserves, conventional financing on individual units can become difficult or unavailable. Cash buyers are unaffected, but buyers using jumbo conventional financing — which is common at Longboat Key price points — need to verify the building's compliance status with their lender before they're deep into a contract.

I flag this early. It's one of the first things I check when a buyer has a specific building in mind.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is Florida's SB 4D condo milestone inspection law?

Florida SB 4D, passed in 2022 after the Surfside collapse, requires condominium buildings three stories or taller that are 30 years old or older to undergo a milestone structural inspection. Phase 1 is a visual inspection by a licensed engineer or architect. If Phase 1 finds substantial structural deterioration, Phase 2 — a more detailed engineering review — is required. Associations that fail to complete inspections on schedule face serious legal and financial consequences.

Do Longboat Key condos have to have milestone inspections?

Yes. Many of Longboat Key's established condo buildings are three stories or taller and over 30 years old, which makes them subject to Florida's SB 4D milestone inspection mandate. As of early 2026, inspections are actively underway on the Key. The Longboat Key Observer reported in April 2026 that buildings are generally in sound structural shape — but the cost to complete inspections and address any findings is real. Every buyer should verify the specific inspection status of their target building before going under contract.

What is a SIRS and how does it affect a condo purchase on Longboat Key?

A SIRS — Structural Integrity Reserve Study — is a mandatory engineering assessment that quantifies what it will cost to repair and maintain a building's critical structural components over time. Under SB 4D, Florida condo associations must now fully fund their reserves based on the SIRS — no more waiving reserve contributions. For buyers, this means higher monthly HOA fees and a reduced risk of surprise special assessments, but you need to request the current SIRS and confirm the association's reserve funding percentage before you close.

How do I find out if a Longboat Key condo has a pending special assessment?

Under Florida law, sellers must disclose known pending special assessments in the purchase contract. But "pending" means the board has formally voted on it — a discussion that hasn't been voted on doesn't trigger that requirement. Request board meeting minutes for the past 24 months, the current budget, the SIRS, and a Certificate of Association Status. Any approved or actively discussed assessments will appear in those documents. Don't rely solely on the seller's verbal disclosure.

Will my lender require milestone inspection documentation for a Longboat Key condo?

Increasingly, yes. Fannie Mae and Freddie Mac updated their condo project approval guidelines to address structural integrity requirements after Surfside. For conventional financing on a condo in a building subject to SB 4D, lenders may require proof that the association is in compliance — including milestone inspection completion and SIRS-based reserve funding. If a building is behind on its inspection or significantly underfunded on reserves, financing on individual units can become difficult or unavailable. This is one of the first things I verify for buyers looking at Longboat Key condos.
