After the Surfside condominium collapse in 2021, Florida overhauled its condo safety laws. The result—Senate Bill 4D, signed in 2022 and updated in 2023—created new mandatory inspection timelines and reserve funding requirements for condo associations statewide. For buyers in Clearwater Beach, where some towers have been standing since the 1970s, the implications are still playing out right now.

Here's what you need to know before you fall in love with a unit.

How SB 4D Works — The Short Version

SB 4D requires condo associations for buildings three stories or taller to complete a two-phase structural inspection process.

Phase 1 is a visual inspection by a licensed engineer or architect. For buildings within three miles of a coastline that were 30 or more years old, Phase 1 was due by December 31, 2024. That's the vast majority of Clearwater Beach.

Phase 2 kicks in only if Phase 1 finds evidence of substantial structural deterioration. When triggered, the association has 365 days to complete a full, detailed structural inspection with engineering recommendations and a remediation plan.

SB 4D also requires every association to complete and fund a Structural Integrity Reserve Study (SIRS)—a detailed financial analysis covering the cost of maintaining, repairing, and replacing major structural components over time. Starting January 1, 2025, associations can no longer vote to waive reserve contributions. Full funding is now mandatory.

That last part is the one quietly reshaping the Clearwater Beach condo market.

Why This Matters on Clearwater Beach Specifically

Clearwater Beach has an abundance of older mid-rise and high-rise condo towers—many built between 1970 and 1995. These are the buildings most directly affected by SB 4D's inspection timelines and the ones where reserve underfunding is most common.

For years, many associations kept HOA fees low by waiving reserves through annual membership votes. It was legal. Owners liked paying less per month. The result is that some buildings now have SIRS reports showing shortfalls measured in the hundreds of thousands—or more.

When the SIRS shortfall is significant, the association has two options: raise monthly dues substantially or levy a special assessment. For buyers entering these buildings, that cost exposure becomes their problem the moment they close.

I've had clients receive special assessment notices within months of buying a Clearwater Beach condo. One was for structural concrete repairs. Another involved seawall work that hadn't been fully disclosed at offer. Neither was visible in the listing. This is why you ask before you offer—not after.

Your SB 4D Due Diligence Checklist for Clearwater Beach

When you're evaluating a condo on Clearwater Beach, here's what to request—and what the answers should tell you:

1. Has Phase 1 been completed?
For coastal buildings 30+ years old, it was required by December 31, 2024. If it hasn't been done, the association is out of compliance—and that creates significant issues for your lender's condo project review.

2. Did Phase 1 trigger a Phase 2?
If yes, ask for the Phase 2 report. Phase 2 findings reveal actual structural conditions and engineering recommendations. This document tells you what repairs are needed and approximately what they'll cost—before you're committed.

3. What does the SIRS say?
Request the full Structural Integrity Reserve Study. The key number is the reserve funding level as a percentage of the fully funded balance. Under 70% is cause for concern. Under 50% is a serious red flag that warrants careful underwriting—and honest conversations about your risk tolerance.

4. Are there any pending or recently levied special assessments?
Florida law requires sellers to disclose known special assessments, but "known" has a specific legal definition. An assessment being discussed at board level—but not yet formally voted on—may not legally require disclosure. Your agent and real estate attorney can help you ask the right questions to surface what isn't automatically revealed.

5. What are the monthly HOA contributions after SIRS compliance?
Associations that were waiving reserves and are now fully funding them have raised dues—sometimes by hundreds of dollars per month. That affects your carrying cost and the building's buyer pool going forward.

6. Is the building currently FHA or VA approved?
Non-warrantable condos—including those with significant reserve underfunding or active special assessments—can lose FHA and VA approval. Confirm your loan type is eligible before you fall in love with a unit.

7. Does the building qualify for conventional financing?
Fannie Mae and Freddie Mac have their own condo project review requirements. A building with pending litigation, more than 15% of units delinquent on HOA dues, or significant deferred maintenance may not be eligible. Your lender will order a condo questionnaire during underwriting, but it's far better to surface these issues before you're under contract.

What the Numbers Actually Look Like

To give you a sense of the stakes: a Clearwater Beach condo tower with 100 units and a $2 million SIRS shortfall would need each owner to contribute an average of $20,000—either through a lump-sum special assessment or substantially higher monthly dues spread over several years.

On a $900,000 beach condo, that's a 2.2% cost hit that didn't show up anywhere in the purchase price. On a $650,000 unit, same story.

The buildings with the cleanest SIRS reports are generally the ones with consistently higher HOA fees that have been funding reserves all along. Newer buildings—those constructed after 2000—are in better shape on average. But a high HOA fee isn't automatically a red flag. Sometimes it's the opposite: evidence that the association is well-managed and financially stable.

The building that looks like a deal because of a lower list price and low monthly dues may be priced that way for a reason.

What a Healthy Building Looks Like

Not every Clearwater Beach tower is a risk. Many buildings have completed their Phase 1 inspections, received clean reports, and are meeting SIRS-required contributions without drama. Some have recently completed major capital improvements—seawall work, parking garage repairs, roof replacements—that reduce near-term assessment risk significantly.

When I'm walking a buyer through a Clearwater Beach condo search, we look at the full picture: HOA fee versus what's included, the SIRS funding level, any recent special assessments already levied and paid, the building's age and construction type, and what the Phase 1 report found. The goal is to find buildings where the financial health matches the asking price.

A clean Phase 1, a well-funded SIRS, and a reserve study with no major structural concerns: that's the green light. A Phase 1 that triggered Phase 2, a reserve fund at 35%, and a board that just approved a six-figure special assessment: that's a very different conversation—and probably a different building.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is the SB 4D deadline for Clearwater Beach condos?

For buildings three stories or taller that are 30 or more years old and within three miles of a coastline—which includes most of Clearwater Beach—Phase 1 milestone inspections were required by December 31, 2024. Buildings 30+ years old elsewhere in Florida had until December 31, 2025. If Phase 1 identifies substantial structural deterioration, Phase 2 must be completed within 365 days of that report.

Can I still get a mortgage on a Clearwater Beach condo with SB 4D issues?

It depends on the severity of the issues and your loan type. Buildings with significant reserve underfunding, active structural concerns, or pending special assessments may not qualify for FHA, VA, or conventional financing through Fannie Mae or Freddie Mac. Jumbo portfolio loans from local lenders sometimes offer more flexibility, but at higher rates. Always confirm financing eligibility before going under contract.

How do I request condo documents as a buyer in Florida?

Under Florida law, you're entitled to receive the association's governing documents, most recent budget, reserve study, and meeting minutes as part of the condo resale package. There's typically a fee of $150–$250 for the package. You should also request the Phase 1 and Phase 2 inspection reports directly—those aren't always included in the standard resale package automatically.

What's the difference between a milestone inspection and a four-point inspection?

A milestone inspection under SB 4D is a structural assessment of the entire building conducted by a licensed engineer or architect. A four-point inspection—which your insurance company may require—covers only four systems: roof, electrical, plumbing, and HVAC. They serve different purposes and aren't interchangeable. Both may be relevant when buying a Clearwater Beach condo.

Are Clearwater Beach condos covered by SB 4D if they're only two stories tall?

No. SB 4D applies to residential condo buildings that are three stories or taller. Two-story buildings are not subject to the milestone inspection or SIRS requirements. However, all Florida condo associations—regardless of building height—remain subject to maintenance obligations and reserve requirements under other Florida statutes.
