Florida's homeowners insurance market is in active transition in 2026: Citizens Property Insurance has slashed its policy count to roughly 270,000 through aggressive depopulation, private carriers are re-entering the state, and many Pinellas County homeowners are receiving non-renewal notices or being moved to new carriers — sometimes with premium swings of 10–20% in either direction. The Nuanced Truth About Florida's Insurance Market in 2026

You may have heard that rates are dropping. That part is true — for some people. Citizens Property Insurance recommended rate cuts of up to 14% for many of its remaining policyholders heading into 2026, and private carriers have been cautiously returning to Florida after years of retreating. The Tallahassee reforms seem to be doing some of what they were designed to do.

But here's where it gets complicated.

Citizens has also been aggressively depopulating — shrinking its policy count from over 1.4 million a few years ago down to roughly 270,000 today. If you're one of the homeowners being moved to a private carrier through that process, your experience might look very different. Depopulation transfers are supposed to happen at rates equal to or lower than your Citizens premium, but "equal or lower" in year one doesn't mean stable in year two or three. And private carrier underwriting is stricter — older roofs, prior claims, and certain construction types that Citizens accepted are getting declined.

The non-renewal crisis from private carriers hasn't fully resolved either. Some insurers are still exiting certain Florida markets or tightening underwriting so aggressively that functionally the same result — a homeowner scrambling for coverage — is happening either way.

So when I say the picture is "mixed," I mean it: if you bought a newer home in the last 10 years with a hip roof and impact windows, 2026 might be genuinely good news for your insurance costs. If you own a 20-year-old flat-roof condo or a 1960s ranch with an older shingle roof, you may still be navigating a very challenging coverage environment.

How This Shows Up in Your St. Petersburg Real Estate Deal

If You're Selling

The first thing to understand: Florida's Seller's Property Disclosure form requires you to disclose known material defects and facts that affect the property's insurability. If your home has had a non-renewal, a claims history, or a roof condition that caused coverage issues, that's material information. Your buyer's lender is going to find out anyway — their underwriter will require proof of insurance before the loan funds — so disclosing it upfront is always the better play.

What you can do before you list to get ahead of this:

  • Get a wind mitigation inspection — if you haven't had one, this is often the single best move a St. Pete seller can make. More on this below.
  • Know your roof's age and condition. Lenders typically require roofs to have remaining useful life. If yours is 15+ years old, price accordingly or get it replaced before listing.
  • Shop insurance yourself before listing — knowing what a buyer will be quoted helps you price and negotiate more confidently.

One thing I tell every seller who asks me about this: an insurance surprise during the inspection period is one of the most common reasons deals fall apart in Pinellas County right now. If there's an issue, you want to know about it — and address it — before you're under contract.

If You're Buying

Buyers in St. Petersburg have insurance leverage they often don't use. During your inspection period, you have the right to investigate the property's insurability — and you should, before you waive any contingencies.

Ask these three questions on every offer you write:

  1. What is the age and condition of the roof? Florida carriers are notorious for declining homes with roofs over 15 years, and some won't touch anything over 10 on certain policy types. Get this number before you fall in love with the house.
  2. Has the property had any insurance claims in the last 5 years? The seller's disclosure should cover this, but ask your agent to pull the CLUE report (Comprehensive Loss Underwriting Exchange) if there's any question. Water damage claims, in particular, can follow a property and make coverage difficult to secure.
  3. What is the current insurance carrier, and are they staying? If the property is on Citizens and being depopulated, or if the current carrier is one of the ones in flux, you want to know what you'll actually be paying — not what the seller is paying today.

Get a real insurance quote — not a ballpark, an actual quote — before your inspection period closes. Your lender is going to require it before closing anyway. Doing it early protects you from an unpleasant surprise when you're already committed.

Got a Citizens Depopulation Letter? Here's What to Do

If you're a homeowner in Pinellas County who received a Citizens depopulation notice, you have choices — but the window to act is short. Citizens gives you 30 days to opt out and remain on their policy. If you don't opt out, you're transferred.

Here's how to think through it:

  • Compare the offered premium carefully. The private carrier's rate has to be within 20% of your Citizens premium at the time of offer. But compare coverage terms too — not just the premium number.
  • Check the private carrier's financial stability rating. Demotech ratings (Florida uses these instead of AM Best for many carriers) give you a sense of whether the company is financially sound.
  • If you're planning to sell in the next 1–2 years, the transfer may actually help you. A property insured by a recognizable private carrier can be an easier sell than a Citizens policy, which some buyers' agents flag as a yellow flag on insurability.

Opting out of depopulation isn't automatically the right call. Citizens rates are dropping, but Citizens is also supposed to be the insurer of last resort — not a permanent home for properties that have private market options. An independent insurance agent who knows the Florida market (not a captive carrier rep) is your best resource here.

The Wind Mitigation Move That Saves Deals and Premiums

If there's one thing I'd tell every homeowner in St. Petersburg — buyer or seller — it's this: get a wind mitigation inspection.

It costs $75–$150. It takes about an hour. And if your home has favorable features — a hip roof, impact windows or shutters, a reinforced roof deck attachment — it can reduce your annual homeowners insurance premium by 25–45%.

On a home paying $6,000/year in premiums, that's $1,500–$2,700 back in your pocket every year. The report is valid for 5 years and is transferable to a new buyer, which makes it a genuine selling point.

For sellers: a favorable wind mitigation report in your listing materials tells buyers exactly what they're getting into on insurance costs before they make an offer. That transparency reduces friction and can actually support your asking price.

For buyers: if the home doesn't have a current wind mitigation report, request one as part of your inspection or negotiate for the seller to provide one before closing. You'll know your insurance picture before you're committed.

Four-point inspections — which most lenders require on homes over 25 years old — cover the roof, electrical, plumbing, and HVAC and are separate from wind mitigation. If the property is older, plan on needing both. Your insurance agent can clarify which is required for your specific loan type and carrier.

Frequently Asked Questions

Do I have to disclose a homeowners insurance non-renewal when selling my St. Petersburg home?

Yes. Florida's Seller's Property Disclosure form requires you to disclose known material defects and insurance history that could affect the property's insurability. If you received a non-renewal notice due to a claims history or roof condition, this is material information a buyer's lender will likely discover anyway. Disclosing it upfront prevents the deal from collapsing at the 11th hour.

Can a deal fall through because of homeowners insurance in Florida?

Absolutely — and it happens more often than most people expect. If a buyer's lender cannot confirm that the property is insurable before closing, the loan won't fund. Properties with old roofs (15+ years), prior water damage claims, or certain construction types can be declined by multiple carriers. Buyers typically have the inspection period to discover insurance issues and either negotiate a remedy or walk away.

What is Citizens Insurance depopulation in Florida?

Citizens Property Insurance — Florida's insurer of last resort — has been actively moving policyholders to private carriers through a process called depopulation. If you receive a depopulation letter, a private carrier has offered to insure your home at a rate equal to or lower than your Citizens premium. You can opt out within 30 days and remain with Citizens, but opting out may limit your ability to return to Citizens in the future.

How much does a wind mitigation inspection save on homeowners insurance in St. Petersburg FL?

A wind mitigation inspection in St. Petersburg typically costs $75–$150 and can reduce your annual homeowners insurance premium by 25–45% if your home has favorable features — like a hip roof, impact-rated windows or shutters, or a reinforced roof deck attachment. For a home paying $6,000/year in premiums, that's $1,500–$2,700 in annual savings. The inspection report is valid for 5 years and can be transferred to a new buyer.

Are homeowners insurance rates going up or down in St. Petersburg FL in 2026?

It depends on your carrier and coverage path. Citizens Property Insurance recommended rate cuts of up to 14% for many policyholders entering 2026, and the broader Florida market is stabilizing as private carriers re-enter the state. However, homeowners being transitioned from Citizens to a private carrier through depopulation, or those with older roofs or prior claims, may still see increases of 10–20% or more. The picture is genuinely mixed — your specific situation determines which direction you're headed.
