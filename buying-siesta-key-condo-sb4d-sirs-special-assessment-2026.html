Buying a Siesta Key Condo in 2026: What SB 4D, SIRS Reserves, and Special Assessments Mean Before You Make an Offer

What do SB 4D and SIRS mean for buyers looking at Siesta Key condos in 2026?

Florida's SB 4D law requires structural milestone inspections for condominium buildings 30+ years old, and a mandated Structural Integrity Reserve Study (SIRS) that condos must now fully fund. On Siesta Key — where most beachfront towers were built in the 1970s and 1980s — these requirements are hitting simultaneously, triggering special assessments of $50,000 to $400,000+ per unit in some buildings. Before making an offer on any older Siesta Key condo, buyers must review the SIRS, milestone inspection status, reserve funding levels, and HOA financials — because these documents will determine your true all-in cost, your ability to get a mortgage, and your exit strategy.

By Amanda LeGault, LeGault Luxe Group | September 28, 2026

There are two Siesta Key condo markets right now.

In one of them, luxury beachfront units are still selling at record numbers — a waterfront estate just closed at $21.95 million cash this month, the highest residential sale in Sarasota city history. The top end of this market is active, competitive, and not going anywhere.

In the other market, some older beachfront towers are sitting with price reductions of 20–40% from their 2022 peaks. Not because of location — the sand is still the finest in the world. Because of a three-letter acronym that wasn't on most buyers' radar two years ago: SB 4D.

If you're looking at a condo on Siesta Key right now, understanding SB 4D — and its companion requirement, the Structural Integrity Reserve Study — isn't optional due diligence. It's the difference between a smart purchase and a $200,000 surprise in year two.

Here's exactly what you need to know before you make an offer.

What SB 4D Actually Requires (And Why Siesta Key Is Ground Zero)

Florida passed Senate Bill 4D in 2022 following the collapse of Champlain Towers South in Surfside. The law targets older condominium buildings that had — for decades — been allowed to defer structural maintenance and waive reserve funding through annual owner votes.

That practice is now illegal.

Under SB 4D, any condominium building that is three stories or taller and 30 years or older must undergo a milestone structural inspection — a two-phase process:

  • Phase 1 — A licensed architect or structural engineer performs a visual inspection of the building's primary structural components. If no significant concerns are found, inspection is complete.
  • Phase 2 — If Phase 1 reveals substantial structural deterioration, Phase 2 kicks in: a hands-on engineering assessment using destructive or nondestructive testing. This is where costs escalate and where special assessments often originate.

Siesta Key is, as local agents like to say bluntly, the epicenter of this issue. The island's beachfront towers were predominantly built in the 1970s and 1980s — which means most of them cleared the 30-year threshold a decade or two ago. They've been absorbing the salt air and storm exposure of Gulf Coast living for 40–50 years. Phase 2 findings in these buildings are not rare.

SIRS: The Reserve Funding Mandate That Changes the Math

Running parallel to the milestone inspection requirement is the Structural Integrity Reserve Study — or SIRS. Every Florida condominium association was required to complete one by December 31, 2024, and they must now fund reserves accordingly. Fully.

Before this law, Florida condo associations had an escape valve: owners could vote annually to waive reserve funding. Many did, year after year, because it kept monthly HOA fees lower. The deferred maintenance piled up silently.

The SIRS mandate closes that valve permanently.

The study quantifies the cost of repairing or replacing a building's structural components — roof, foundation, load-bearing walls, waterproofing, plumbing, and more — over a 30-year horizon, and it tells the association how much they need to be collecting each year to cover those costs when they come due. If the reserve fund is dramatically underfunded against the SIRS requirements, the association has three options:

  1. Rapidly increase monthly HOA dues
  2. Issue a special assessment — a lump-sum charge billed directly to unit owners
  3. Some combination of both

On older Siesta Key towers, the gap between actual reserves and SIRS-required reserves has been staggering. I've seen special assessments on beachfront units running from $80,000 to north of $400,000 per unit — not theoretical numbers. Real ones.

What This Means for Your Offer, Your Financing, and Your Risk

The offer price isn't your all-in cost

A condo listed at $1.2 million in a building carrying a $180,000 pending special assessment isn't a $1.2 million purchase. It's a $1.38 million purchase — and that's before your closing costs, your title insurance (which in Sarasota County buyers typically pay), and your first year of carrying costs.

The assessment is always negotiable as part of the offer. You can ask the seller to pay it at closing, credit you at closing, or you can accept it yourself — but you need to know it exists and quantify it before you make any number.

The financing angle most buyers miss

Here's what doesn't come up enough in the online forums about SB 4D: the mortgage problem.

After the Surfside collapse, Fannie Mae and Freddie Mac substantially tightened their condominium lending guidelines. Both agencies now require lenders to review reserve fund levels, milestone inspection status, and any active special assessments before approving a loan. A building with significant deferred maintenance, an ongoing Phase 2 investigation, or reserves funded well below SIRS requirements may fail warrantability — which means conventional lenders won't issue loans on any unit in that building, regardless of what shape your unit is in.

At Siesta Key's price points, you're almost certainly in jumbo loan territory anyway. Jumbo lenders have their own underwriting standards layered on top, and they can be stricter. Portfolio lenders — community banks and private mortgage banks that keep loans on their own books — often have more flexibility here, but they'll still want to see the SIRS and the financials.

The bottom line: before you fall in love with a unit, have your lender's condo questionnaire sent to the association. The answers to that questionnaire will tell you whether conventional financing is even on the table.

Insurance compounds the picture

Many Siesta Key beachfront towers sit in FEMA AE and VE flood zones — the highest-risk coastal designations. VE zone properties require separate flood insurance, often through private carriers since NFIP coverage caps out well below the values involved here. Wind insurance through Citizens Property Insurance has faced substantial premium increases, and Citizens is actively non-renewing policies in high-value coastal zones as part of their depopulation efforts.

Before you close, you'll also need to confirm the association's master policy — what it covers, what the building deductible is, and whether individual unit coverage layers on top. A four-point inspection (roof, HVAC, plumbing, electrical) will likely be required by your insurer to bind coverage. A wind mitigation inspection can meaningfully reduce your premium — on a luxury condo in a high-wind zone, the savings can be $5,000–$15,000 annually.

Factor all of this in when you're comparing buildings. Two condos at the same list price can have insurance costs that differ by $30,000 per year based on flood zone, building age, and mitigation features.

The Due Diligence Documents You Need Before You Offer

Don't wait until you're under contract to ask for these. Request them before you make an offer — the answers will tell you what to offer and whether to offer at all.

  • Current year operating budget and reserve fund balance — How much is in reserves? What does the SIRS say they need?
  • The SIRS itself — Full report, not a summary. Look at the reserve balance shortfall and the 30-year projection.
  • Milestone inspection report — Phase 1 completed? If Phase 2 was required, what did it find? What remediation was recommended?
  • Last 12–24 months of board meeting minutes — This is where special assessments get discussed before they're formally issued. It's also where you'll see patterns of deferred decisions.
  • Any pending or recently issued special assessments — Amount, per-unit breakdown, payment structure (lump sum or installment), and what it covers.
  • Master insurance policy declarations page — Coverage amounts, building deductible, carrier, and any exclusions.
  • Condo questionnaire responses — Ask your lender to send the standard Fannie Mae form to the association before you remove your financing contingency.

In Florida, sellers are required to provide the condo documents within a specified timeframe after an executed contract — but reviewing them before you offer gives you a cleaner negotiating position. It's also the difference between a due-diligence period spent confirming your decision versus a due-diligence period spent unwinding it.

Every situation is different, and what these documents reveal will shape what I advise clients to do — whether that's adjusting the offer, negotiating for the seller to pay the assessment, or walking away from a building that doesn't pencil. The only way to know which bucket you're in is to see the numbers.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is SB 4D and does it apply to condos on Siesta Key?

SB 4D is Florida's 2022 condo safety law requiring milestone structural inspections for all condominium buildings three stories or taller that are 30 or more years old. It absolutely applies to Siesta Key — the island's beachfront towers were predominantly built in the 1970s and 1980s, putting most of them squarely in the compliance window. If a building fails Phase 1 visual inspection, it must proceed to a Phase 2 structural engineering assessment, and owners are responsible for funding any required repairs.

What is a SIRS and why does it matter when buying a Siesta Key condo?

A Structural Integrity Reserve Study (SIRS) is a mandated financial and engineering analysis that quantifies the cost of repairing or replacing a building's structural components — roof, foundation, load-bearing walls, waterproofing systems, and more — over a 30-year horizon. Florida law now requires condos to fully fund these reserves, not waive them as was common practice before 2022. If a Siesta Key building is underfunded against its SIRS, the gap typically becomes a special assessment — a one-time charge billed directly to unit owners.

Can I get a mortgage on a Siesta Key condo that has a special assessment or underfunded reserves?

Possibly, but it's complicated. Fannie Mae and Freddie Mac updated their condo lending guidelines following the 2021 Surfside collapse, and both agencies now scrutinize reserve funding levels closely. A building with significant deferred maintenance, an active Phase 2 milestone inspection, or reserves funded below the SIRS-required threshold may fail warrantability — meaning conventional lenders won't lend on any unit in the building, regardless of the individual unit's condition. At Siesta Key's luxury price points, this almost always means a jumbo loan, which has its own layered underwriting requirements. Your lender needs to see the current budget, SIRS, and any active or pending special assessments before issuing a commitment.

How much are special assessments running on Siesta Key condo towers right now?

It varies dramatically by building age, size, and how aggressively reserves were previously underfunded. In 2025 and 2026, some older beachfront towers on Siesta Key have issued special assessments ranging from $50,000 to well over $400,000 per unit — real numbers, not hypotheticals. That range is why older Siesta Key condo prices have softened considerably in certain buildings, while well-funded newer buildings or recently renovated towers hold value. The assessment amount should be disclosed in the HOA financials and meeting minutes — and if it isn't volunteered, you need to ask directly.

What documents should I request before making an offer on a Siesta Key condo?

Before making an offer, request: the current year operating budget and reserve fund balance, the most recent SIRS (Structural Integrity Reserve Study), the milestone inspection report (Phase 1 and Phase 2 if applicable), the last 12–24 months of board meeting minutes, any pending or recently issued special assessments, and the current master insurance policy including building coverage and deductible amounts. In Florida, sellers and associations are required to provide many of these upon request — but asking before the offer, not after, gives you real negotiating leverage.
