The FEMA 50% Rule on Siesta Key: What Buyers of Older Waterfront Homes Need to Know Before Closing (2026)

How does the FEMA 50% rule affect buying an older home on Siesta Key?

If the cost of renovating or repairing a home in a Siesta Key flood zone reaches 50% or more of the building's market value, not including the land, Sarasota County requires the entire structure to meet current flood rules, which usually means elevating it or rebuilding. Because land makes up most of a Siesta Key price, a $2 million canal home may have a building value of only $300,000 to $400,000, so a $175,000 remodel can trigger the rule. Run that math, and check the home's permit and damage history, before you make an offer.

By Amanda LeGault | November 28, 2026

The question I hear most from buyers touring older homes along the Siesta Key canals, Roberts Bay, and the Midnight Pass Road corridor isn't about the view. It's this: "Can I actually renovate this, or am I buying a teardown?"

That answer almost always comes down to one rule most buyers have never heard of until they're in a permit office.

Two years after Hurricanes Helene and Milton, and with lot values on the key still setting records (a Gulf-front estate on Higel Avenue sold for a reported $26 million in September), the FEMA 50% rule is now part of every serious offer I write on an older Siesta Key home. Here's how it works and how to run the numbers before you commit.

What the 50% rule actually says

The 50% rule is the plain-English name for FEMA's Substantial Improvement and Substantial Damage standard. Sarasota County enforces it through its floodplain ordinance on every building permit in a Special Flood Hazard Area, which covers essentially all of Siesta Key.

Here's the test:

  • Cost of the work (renovation, addition, or storm repair) is divided by the market value of the structure only, measured before the work or before the damage.
  • If the result is under 50%, the permit moves forward under normal rules.
  • If the result is 50% or more, the entire building must be brought into compliance with current flood requirements. In practice, that means raising the lowest floor to the required elevation or demolishing and rebuilding.

Two details matter most on Siesta Key:

Land doesn't count. The county's form specifically uses the depreciated value of the building only, not the lot, pool, dock, or landscaping.

You can challenge the value. The county will use an adjusted assessed value unless you submit an actual cash value (ACV) appraisal of the structure from a licensed appraiser. Staff review it to make sure it describes the building and excludes land. A well-supported appraisal is often the single most valuable document in an older-home purchase here.

Why Siesta Key buyers get caught

On the mainland, the house is usually the bulk of the price. On Siesta Key, it's the opposite. You're paying for the water, the beach access, and the dirt.

Here's a realistic example for a 1970s ground-level canal home:

Purchase price$2,100,000

That "light refresh" is already over the line. Once triggered, the conversation shifts from new countertops to elevating the house or starting over.

Your numbers will be different. Building values vary widely by age, size, and condition, and every contractor bid is its own conversation. But the pattern holds across the island: the older and lower the house, the smaller your renovation budget.

Where the rule bites hardest on Siesta Key

Ground-level canal and bayfront homes. The Siesta Key canal area and Roberts Bay waterfront are full of mid-century homes built well below today's required elevation. These are the properties most likely to be a 50% rule purchase, whether the buyer knows it or not.

Gulf-front and near-Gulf homes in Zone VE. If the home sits in a VE zone, a Coastal A Zone, or seaward of the state's Coastal Construction Control Line (CCCL), a substantial improvement also falls under coastal high-hazard construction standards, including engineering certification and strict limits on enclosed space below the elevated floor (the county's VE certificate caps enclosures at 299 square feet for single-family homes). Pair this with our guide to Siesta Key flood zones AE, VE, and X before you shortlist Gulf-front properties.

Homes repaired after Helene and Milton. If a home was damaged in 2024, the county may have made a substantial damage determination. If repairs were made, you want to know whether they were permitted, how they were valued, and whether the home is now compliant or still carrying a determination that affects what you can do next.

Small, older condo buildings. The rule measures the whole building, so remodeling a single unit in a large tower near Crescent Beach or Point of Rocks rarely triggers it. Two-story and low-rise buildings with ground-floor units are a different story, especially if the association made major storm repairs. Your pre-offer checklist

This is the due diligence I walk every older-home buyer through on Siesta Key. Most of it should happen before you sign, not during the inspection period.

  1. Pull the elevation certificate. Sarasota County posts many elevation certificates online. Compare the lowest floor elevation to the current requirement for the flood zone.
  2. Pull the permit history. Look for post-2024 storm repair permits, substantial damage letters, and any prior 50% rule worksheets on file for the address.
  3. Ask about cumulative improvements. Ask the county floodplain administrator how prior permits on the property count toward the threshold, so a past owner's work doesn't eat into your budget.
  4. Review the seller's flood disclosures. Florida sellers must disclose known flood insurance claims and federal flood assistance on the property. A past claim is a signal to dig deeper.
  5. Order a structure-only ACV appraisal. If you plan meaningful work, this can raise your ceiling. Make it a contract contingency if you can.
  6. Get a contractor's scope and bid. Price everything you actually want to do. Interior finishes, windows, roofs, and mechanicals generally count toward the total.
  7. Price the insurance. Older below-elevation homes can carry heavy flood premiums. A four-point inspection and a wind mitigation report will also shape your homeowners quote. See our Siesta Key flood insurance guide for real carrying cost ranges.

Three ways to play it

Once you have the numbers, every older Siesta Key home falls into one of three strategies:

  • Renovate under the cap. Keep the work below 50%, prioritize what matters most, and enjoy the home as is. This works well for lock-and-leave second homes and vacation rentals with a good layout.
  • Elevate the existing structure. Sometimes possible for solid block homes, but it's engineering-heavy and not always cheaper than starting over.
  • Buy it as a lot. Price the offer on land value, plan a new elevated home, and treat the existing house as a demolition cost. On the Gulf side, CCCL permitting from the Florida Department of Environmental Protection adds time to the schedule.

The worst outcome is buying with a renovation plan, closing, and finding out at permitting that the plan doesn't work. That's the scenario I help buyers avoid, and it's why I run the 50% math before we write an offer, not after.

Frequently Asked Questions

Does the FEMA 50% rule include land value on Siesta Key?

No. Sarasota County compares the cost of the improvement or repair to the market value of the building only, excluding land and site improvements. On Siesta Key, where land is often 70% to 85% of the sale price, that makes the threshold much lower than most buyers expect.

Can I get my own appraisal to raise the 50% threshold in Sarasota County?

Yes. Sarasota County lets owners submit an actual cash value appraisal of the structure, prepared by a licensed appraiser, instead of relying on the adjusted assessed value. County staff review it to confirm it describes the building and excludes land. A well-supported appraisal can meaningfully raise your renovation budget.

Does the 50% rule apply to my Siesta Key condo renovation?

The rule is measured against the whole building, so a single-unit remodel in a large Gulf-front tower rarely triggers it. It matters more for small, older low-rise condo buildings, especially ones repaired after Hurricanes Helene and Milton, where the association's repairs may have been measured against the building's value.

What happens if a Siesta Key home is declared substantially damaged?

The entire structure must be brought into compliance with current flood requirements, which usually means elevating it to the required elevation or demolishing and rebuilding. In Zone VE, Coastal A Zones, and seaward of the Coastal Construction Control Line, coastal high-hazard construction standards also apply.

How do I find out if a Siesta Key home was substantially damaged before I buy?

Ask the seller in writing, pull the Sarasota County permit history and any substantial damage letters for the address, and request the elevation certificate. Florida sellers must also disclose known flood insurance claims and federal flood assistance on the property.

The bottom line

On Siesta Key, you're buying the land, and the FEMA 50% rule makes sure you remember it. Know the structure value, know your renovation ceiling, and know your plan before you write the offer.
