Anna Maria Island Vacation Rental Regulations 2026: What Buyers and Investors Need to Know Before Closing

By Amanda LeGault  |  October 8, 2026

What are the vacation rental regulations on Anna Maria Island in 2026?

Anna Maria Island has existing city-level short-term rental restrictions — registration requirements, quiet hours ordinances, and parking caps — that differ across the three island municipalities: the City of Anna Maria, Holmes Beach, and Bradenton Beach. On top of these city rules, Manatee County commissioners are actively considering a new countywide vacation rental ordinance, enabled by Florida's 2025 SB 280 legislation. As of October 2026, that county-level vote is unresolved, leaving buyers of investment property on AMI navigating genuine regulatory uncertainty that directly affects cap rates, permit transferability, and purchase negotiations.

If you're looking at an Anna Maria Island property as a vacation rental investment right now, you're buying into one of the most tightly regulated short-term rental environments in Florida — and the rules are actively changing.

That's not a reason to walk away. AMI is one of the most sought-after vacation rental destinations in the country, and high-quality properties here are historically hard to find and harder to replicate. But it is a reason to understand exactly what you're buying before your earnest money is at risk.

Here's what's in place today, what Manatee County is proposing, and how to factor all of it into your offer.

What the existing city ordinances already require

Anna Maria Island is made up of three separate municipalities — the City of Anna Maria, Holmes Beach, and Bradenton Beach — and each has its own vacation rental rules. This is where most buyers get tripped up: they research "Anna Maria Island regulations" and get a mixed picture because they're unknowingly blending three different sets of rules.

Here's the city-by-city breakdown as it stands today:

City of Anna Maria

The northernmost city has some of the strictest existing rules on the island. Rentals require registration with the city, and the ordinance enforces quiet hours from 10 PM to 8 AM. Parking is capped — typically limited to the number of spaces on-site — and overflow parking on public streets is not counted toward your occupancy. The city actively enforces violations, and fines are not symbolic.

Holmes Beach

Holmes Beach has a rental registration requirement and occupancy limits tied to bedroom count and square footage — not just however many guests will fit. Their ordinance includes noise restrictions and limits on the number of rentals that can operate within proximity of each other in certain residential zones. If you're buying in Holmes Beach, verify whether your target property's rental registration is current and in good standing.

Bradenton Beach

Bradenton Beach similarly requires vacation rental permits and has operational rules around noise, trash, and parking. The permit situation here is particularly worth scrutinizing at closing — more on that below.

The practical takeaway: you can't apply one set of rules island-wide. The property address determines which city's ordinance applies, and you need to read that city's current code — or have someone do it for you before you make an offer.

What Manatee County is proposing — and why it matters even for city properties

In June 2026, Manatee County commissioners began formal consideration of a new countywide short-term rental ordinance. This was made possible by Florida's 2025 SB 280, which gave counties significantly more regulatory authority over vacation rentals than they previously held. Before SB 280, state preemption largely blocked local governments from tightening STR rules beyond what was already in place. That barrier is now gone.

The proposed Manatee County ordinance has included elements like registration requirements, potential caps on the number of permitted rental nights per year, occupancy limits based on property size, and noise and parking enforcement standards. Commissioners have been split on the scope of the regulations, and as of this writing, no final vote has been taken.

What This Means for Your Investment Decision

Properties within the incorporated cities of Anna Maria, Holmes Beach, and Bradenton Beach are governed by city ordinances — not the county. But county regulations can still affect unincorporated parcels on and around the island. More importantly, the county vote signals the direction of regulatory pressure across all of Manatee County. If you're also looking at properties in Bradenton Beach, Longboat Key's north end, or anywhere in unincorporated Manatee County, the county outcome matters directly.

For investors, the unresolved vote creates a real underwriting question: are you pricing this property based on today's rental income, or on what that income looks like if minimum night stays increase or seasonal caps are imposed? That's a number you need to model before you close, not after.

The permit transferability question — ask this before you make an offer

This is one of the most consequential and least-asked questions in an AMI purchase transaction.

In some cases, a vacation rental permit or city registration is tied to the property address and conveys to a new owner at closing. In other cases — and this is critical — the permit is issued to the individual operator or owner, and it does not transfer. If the seller's permit doesn't convey and the city has imposed a cap or freeze on new registrations (which Holmes Beach has done at various points), you could close on a property assuming you're buying a rental business and discover post-closing that you need to apply for a new permit that may not be available.

What to ask for in writing before going under contract:

  • Is the current rental registration issued to the property or to the seller personally?
  • Is the registration current and free of violations or pending fines?
  • Does the city allow new rental registrations, or is there a current freeze or cap?
  • Is there a queue or waiting period for new permits if the current one doesn't transfer?

Your real estate attorney should review the permit documentation as part of due diligence. This isn't a question to leave to the listing description.

The real number: running an honest cap rate on an AMI investment

Anna Maria Island properties generate some of the strongest gross rental income in the state. A well-positioned 4–5 bedroom home on or near the water can gross $80,000 to $150,000 or more in annual rental revenue. Those numbers are real. But gross income is not your return — and on AMI specifically, the gap between gross and net is wider than most buyers realize when they first see a Vacasa or VRBO income report.

Here's a realistic net income model on a $2.5M AMI property generating $110,000 gross:

  • Property management fees (30%): −$33,000
  • Specialty STR insurance (coastal/wind): −$14,000
  • Manatee County property taxes (approx. 1% non-homestead): −$25,000
  • Cleaning, maintenance, supplies: −$12,000
  • Platform fees, utilities, landscaping: −$8,000
  • HOA (if applicable): −$3,000–$8,000
  • Estimated net income: $10,000–$18,000

That's a net yield of roughly 0.4–0.7% on purchase price — before mortgage debt service if you're financing. Many AMI investors are buying for appreciation, lifestyle use, and long-term wealth preservation rather than short-term yield, and that's a completely valid thesis. But know which thesis you're making before you're under contract.

On insurance specifically: standard homeowners policies — including Citizens Property Insurance — typically exclude or severely limit coverage for actively rented vacation properties. Citizens won't insure a property as a primary residence when it's operating as a commercial STR. You'll need a specialty short-term rental policy or a commercial landlord policy with STR coverage — and given AMI's coastal wind exposure, quotes will reflect that. Budget for a wind mitigation inspection and a four-point inspection, both of which carriers require and which can meaningfully improve your premium if the property qualifies.

How to factor regulatory uncertainty into your negotiation

The unresolved Manatee County vote isn't just a background fact — it's a negotiating variable.

Sellers who have been renting under the current rule set know that a tighter county ordinance could reduce their property's income profile. If you're a buyer, that uncertainty is a legitimate basis for either a price adjustment or specific contingency language in the contract. A few approaches worth discussing with your agent:

  • Request current rental income documentation — actual STR platform statements, not seller-prepared summaries. Verify occupancy rates, average nightly rate, and seasonal patterns.
  • Model two scenarios — one under current regulations, one under a stricter ordinance with a minimum 7-night stay and capped occupancy nights. What does the income look like in each?
  • Ask about the rental history under current ordinance — a property that already performs well within existing AMI restrictions is a more resilient investment than one that depends on a maximum-occupancy, minimum-restriction model.
  • Verify permit status before going hard — make permit transferability confirmation a due diligence item, not an assumption.

Your specific picture depends on the property, the city, and your financial structure — and the only way to get that picture clearly is to run the numbers with someone who works in this market every day.

Frequently Asked Questions

What vacation rental restrictions already exist on Anna Maria Island?

Anna Maria Island has city-level short-term rental restrictions that vary across its three municipalities. The City of Anna Maria requires rental registration and enforces strict quiet hours and parking caps. Holmes Beach has its own registration and occupancy ordinances. Bradenton Beach similarly requires permits and imposes operational rules. These city-level rules exist regardless of what Manatee County ultimately decides at the county level.

What is Manatee County proposing for vacation rental regulation in 2026?

Manatee County commissioners are considering a new countywide vacation rental ordinance, made possible by Florida's 2025 SB 280 legislation that restored counties' authority to regulate short-term rentals. The proposed rules could include registration requirements, minimum night stay mandates, occupancy caps, and noise and parking restrictions for unincorporated Manatee County areas. As of October 2026, commissioners remain split and no final vote has been taken — meaning buyers are making investment decisions under genuine regulatory uncertainty.

Do vacation rental permits transfer when you buy a property on Anna Maria Island?

It depends on the city and the permit type — and this is one of the most important due diligence questions to ask before you make an offer. Some registrations in Holmes Beach and Bradenton Beach are tied to the property address and transfer at closing. Others are personal licenses issued to the individual operator and do not transfer. Buying a property assuming the rental permit conveys — then discovering it doesn't — can fundamentally change the investment thesis. Verify permit transferability in writing before going under contract.

What does homeowners insurance cost for a vacation rental on Anna Maria Island?

Standard homeowners insurance — including Citizens Property Insurance — typically excludes or severely limits coverage when a property is actively rented to guests. For an AMI vacation rental, investors typically need a specialty short-term rental policy or a commercial landlord policy with an STR rider, which can run $8,000–$20,000 or more annually on a $2M–$3M coastal property. Wind mitigation inspections and four-point inspections are required by most carriers and can meaningfully reduce premiums. Factor this cost into your net return calculation from day one.

What is a realistic net return on an Anna Maria Island vacation rental?

Gross rental revenue on AMI properties can look impressive — $80,000 to $150,000+ annually on a $2M–$3M home. But net returns after property management fees (typically 25–35% of gross), specialty insurance, Manatee County property taxes, HOA fees, cleaning, maintenance, and platform fees often land at 0.4–1.5% of purchase price. That's not a dealbreaker for buyers who value long-term appreciation and personal use, but it is a very different picture than the top-line numbers suggest. Run the full net projection — not just the gross — before you decide.
