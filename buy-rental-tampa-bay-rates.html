Should You Buy a Rental in Tampa Bay With Rates Where They Are?

Is it still worth buying a rental property in Tampa Bay with today's mortgage rates?

It can be, if the rent covers your full monthly cost. Investors bought 10.9% of homes in the Tampa–St. Petersburg–Clearwater metro in the first half of 2026, paying a median of $280,000, about 23.3% below the overall market median. They're focusing on price points where rent can carry the mortgage, taxes, insurance, and upkeep. The rate matters only as one line in that math.

By Amanda LeGault, Licensed FL REALTOR® (#SL3506757) | November 3, 2026

If you've been waiting on rates before buying your first rental, you're not alone. It's one of the questions I hear most from newer investors right now.

But here's what the data shows: investors are still buying in Tampa Bay. They're just buying smarter.

According to Realtor.com's October investor report, investors bought 10.9% of all homes sold in the Tampa–St. Pete–Clearwater metro from January through June 2026. That's roughly one in every nine homes.

And they weren't paying top dollar. Their median purchase price was $280,000, about 23.3% below the overall market median.

What Tampa Bay investors are actually buying

Compare that to other Florida markets in the same report:

  • Miami–Fort Lauderdale: 14.7% investor share, $525,000 median (3.3% above market)
  • Orlando: 12.8% share, $372,195 median (about 7% below market)
  • Jacksonville: 11.2% share, $240,000 median (33.3% below market)
  • Tampa Bay: 10.9% share, $280,000 median (23.3% below market)
  • Cape Coral–Fort Myers: 7.9% share, $390,000 median (9.9% above market)
  • Port St. Lucie: 6% share, $283,500 median (27.3% below market)

In Tampa Bay, investors are clearly concentrating on the lower end of the market. That's not an accident.

Lower purchase prices mean smaller loans. Smaller loans mean lower monthly payments. And lower payments are much easier for rent to cover, even at today's rates.

Why the rate isn't the number that decides your deal

I'm not going to tell you rates don't matter. They do. A higher rate means a higher monthly payment.

But on a rental, you're not the one paying that payment. Your tenant is. So the question that matters isn't "Is the rate low enough?"

It's this: Does the rent cover everything, with something left over?

Here's how I walk my clients through it on a $280,000 home:

  1. Down payment: Most investment loans require 15–25% down. At 25%, that's $70,000.
  2. Loan amount: $210,000.
  3. Principal and interest: At an example rate of 6.5% on a 30-year loan, that's about $1,327 a month. Your lender will give you your actual rate.
  4. Property taxes: Rentals don't get Florida's homestead exemption, so budget for the full non-homestead tax bill for that specific property.
  5. Insurance: Homeowners plus flood insurance if the property needs it. In Tampa Bay, this line can swing your whole deal, so get a real quote before you write an offer. (Here's more on what's happening with Florida homeowners insurance right now.)
  6. HOA, maintenance, vacancy, and management: Many investors set aside 10–20% of rent for repairs, vacancies, and management.
  7. Expected rent: Based on current rental comps for that exact area and property type.

Rent minus all of that = your monthly cash flow.

If that number is positive and comfortable, the deal works at today's rate. If rates drop later, you may be able to refinance, depending on your equity and qualifying at that time.

If the number is negative, a rate drop of half a point probably won't fix it. The price, the rent, or the insurance is the real problem.

What waiting actually costs you
A lot of people don't realize that waiting has its own price. While you wait, you're not collecting rent, you're not paying down a loan, and you're not building equity. And if other buyers jump back in when rates move, you may be competing for the same homes at higher prices.

That doesn't mean you should rush. It means you should run the numbers on real properties instead of waiting for a headline to tell you it's time.

What to look for in a Tampa Bay rental right now

When I help investors here, these are the things we check before anything else:

  • Flood zone and elevation. These drive insurance costs, and insurance drives cash flow. (See flood zones AE vs. X explained.)
  • Roof age and major systems. Older roofs can make insurance harder to get or more expensive. (More on the four-point inspection.)
  • HOA rental rules. Some communities restrict leasing, set minimum lease terms, or cap the number of rentals. Always read the documents.
  • Short-term rental rules. City and county rules vary across Tampa, St. Pete, and Clearwater. Never assume a property can be used as a vacation rental.
  • Rent comps for that exact pocket. A few blocks can change what a home rents for.

Every one of these can turn a "good deal" into a money pit, or the other way around. That's where working with someone who knows this market makes a real difference.

Frequently Asked Questions

What percentage of Tampa Bay homes are bought by investors?

Investors bought 10.9% of homes in the Tampa–St. Petersburg–Clearwater metro in the first half of 2026, according to Realtor.com. That figure only counts purchases made through LLCs and trusts, so it doesn't include investors who bought in their own names.

What price range are Tampa Bay investors buying in?

The median investor purchase price in Tampa Bay was $280,000 in early 2026. That's about 23.3% below the overall market median, so investors are focusing on lower-priced homes where rent can cover costs.

Should I wait for mortgage rates to drop before buying a rental property?

Not necessarily. If the rent covers your mortgage, taxes, insurance, and upkeep at today's rate, the property can work now, and a refinance may be possible later. If it doesn't cash flow, a small rate drop usually won't fix it.

How much do I need to put down on an investment property in Florida?

Most conventional investment loans require 15–25% down, depending on the lender and loan type. Talk with a lender who works with investors to confirm your options.

Does a Florida rental property get the homestead exemption?

No. Florida's homestead exemption only applies to your primary residence, so budget for higher property taxes on a rental.
